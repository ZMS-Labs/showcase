Independent projects / ZMS Labs

I direct the work
and review the results.

These are personal AI-assisted experiments, separate from my employer work. AI tools write the code; I choose the problems, direct the work and inspect what comes back. My contribution is direction, domain judgment, design and critical review.

Steno

I work with contracts. I wanted a way to ask what an agreement would actually do in a particular situation, then follow the answer back to the language that supports it.

When an agreement requires notice, how must it be given? What other obligations apply? How do the liability terms affect the answer? Those questions can involve several parts of an agreement at once.

That was the question behind Steno: a contract workstation that keeps the document, its connections and the questions under review together. It’s a personal experiment, still in development. The legal decisions stay with the person reviewing the contract.

SaveBench

With SaveBench, I wanted to see how AI models would approach building a factory in the game Satisfactory. What would they design? What would they change after a failed attempt? Would the factory actually work in the game? The layouts and revisions are part of what makes the experiment interesting to me.

For the trials recorded on 9 September 2026, an AI agent designed the factory. The measuring tool first failed its own check, so nothing was judged. After a repair, the same design fell short of a target in two of its three two-minute measuring windows. Four days later, on a harder challenge with faster belts allowed, factories designed by AI models passed in the game, one of them after revising its own design from a measured failure.

When an agent says it’s done

I wanted AI agents (tools that carry out a multi-step task on their own) to investigate problems properly, use the tools available to them and check whether their changes actually worked. Epistemic Skills grew around those expectations: seventeen written methods an agent can load when a task calls for one, with the source public. When this site was first published, a check found that 14 of the site’s 28 files didn’t match their recorded fingerprints. After the fix, all 28 matched. The task record names the method as Did It Land, one of the seventeen. The comparisons so far haven’t shown whether the methods make agents better.

Fleet Orchestrator is the next problem along: once several agents are working, keeping track of who is doing what and where a task was handed off. I want one screen where I can see that work and step in. Its review gate, the check that decides whether handed-back work can move on, refuses an approval that comes with no reason, reference or finding attached.

Stories and games

Neuraxic comes from wanting a place where a story’s prose, people, places and rules can develop together. I wanted to be able to try a different version without losing track of what I’d already decided. Krewcible explores character design through choices you can edit and recombine. Gridiron is a commentary companion for a football video game, built on a record of what happened on each play. Enaction keeps the person playing separate from the character they choose to play, down to whose memory gets changed.

A shared service

Poiesis is an experimental service that apps can share when they need text or images generated. I wanted it to be clear which file came back and how long it stays available.

The words the projects use

A few words come from the projects themselves, and they mean the same thing wherever they appear.

  • matter (Steno): one contract with its related documents and open questions.
  • Hallmark (Steno): the emblem Steno uses to show a grade and, separately, what kinds of evidence back it up. It’s modeled on the stamp an assayer (someone who tests metal) strikes into metal.
  • Surface Bridge (Fleet Orchestrator): the part that takes a decision about what an agent should do next, turns it into commands for whichever AI tool is running that agent, and reports back how it went.
  • tome (Neuraxic): a story world’s grouping that holds its related ideas, prose and claims.
  • loose leaf (Neuraxic): an element that has not yet found its place.

How I work with AI tools

AI tools write the code. I decide what each project is for and check what comes back. I use AI tools for research, design, testing and writing too, including first drafts of these pages. I direct the action and observe with a critical eye. Some people let the wall of text fly by. I read it.

In SaveBench, an AI model earned the game’s milestones by feeding machines by hand and never built a conveyor belt, even after hand transfers started to cost game time. At first I called that a real flaw. Then I decided SaveBench measures factory design, not whether an AI chooses to automate, so the test became a quota the factory has to keep meeting while nobody touches it.

Other people’s libraries, tools and projects are part of the work too, and the case studies and the Evidence page credit them. These are personal projects. Each case study says what’s real, what’s made-up sample content and what hasn’t been tested yet, and no real agreement or matter record appears anywhere on the site. There’s still plenty to do.
