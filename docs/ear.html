My contribution

The process needed
a usable working record.

For our client, I helped devise and operationalize the multi-year chancery claims process. I drafted the initial complaint and coordinated service, and I served as a contact for claimant counsel.

I built and reconciled working asset and claim information, compared competing equipment claims, and prepared court reports, hearing materials and substantive trial pleadings. I worked with the team through individual court-approved settlements.

Two distinct results / Separate evidence limits

Value from equipment.
A competing claim defeated.

Putting abandoned assets to work

We helped realize and distribute value from abandoned equipment. I remember expectations being low, which made the result especially meaningful for our client. That qualitative account is my recollection; I am not publishing recovery totals, individual distributions or settlement terms.

A separate $17.49m claim challenge

My trial drafting and factual work helped defeat a competing net claim of $17,487,520.30. Our client obtained summary judgment on March 6, 2012, affirmed on September 16, 2013. The amount describes the rejected claim, not money recovered.

Why the process was unusual

A chancery process alongside
a bankruptcy proceeding.

EAR had entered bankruptcy. This was a separate state-court process, outside ordinary bankruptcy claims administration: equipment disputes were consolidated in chancery, an auction was authorized, and claims followed the equipment into the auction proceeds. I helped turn that unusual procedural setting into working records, court materials and coordinated steps for our client.

This is not a claim that I invented a first-ever procedure or administered every creditor neutrally. Our client asserted a blanket-lien position; competing parties asserted their own rights. The opinions do not establish that every competing interest was subordinate.

A separate proceeding / December 19, 2011

Approving a compromise
has its own scope.

The district court affirmed the bankruptcy court’s settlement approval. It reviewed the compromise, rather than issuing a final ruling on reformation.

The judge considered possible litigation outcomes and the costs and benefits of settlement. That does not settle all competing asset-rights questions.
