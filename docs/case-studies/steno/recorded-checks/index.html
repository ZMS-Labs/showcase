01

Start with a situation.

A focused question is more useful than a general request to “review the contract.”

The fictional assignment

A customer relies on a vendor for a service. After a reported incident, a reviewer needs to know how notice has to be delivered under the agreement and how its indemnity and liability language is drafted.

The clauses below were written for this page. They are not taken from a real agreement.

Questions to carry into the review

  1. Does incident notice point to the correct delivery mechanism?
  2. Does the indemnity wording leave a consequential ambiguity?
  3. How is the liability limit expressed?
  4. What starts the notice clock, and which claims are covered?
02

Follow the wording to the finding.

Switch between two drafts to see what the two checks returned for each. The results were recorded once, on 19 September 2026, and nothing runs live here.

The two checks flag a notice clause that doesn't point to how notice is delivered, and a liability limit that doesn't say how it relates to the indemnity.

Agreement excerpts

Four excerpts, §6 to §9

Initial draft
§ 6Notices

Notices shall be delivered by email to the notice address designated by each party.

§ 7Security Incident

Vendor shall provide written notice of a security incident within 24 hours.

§ 8Indemnity

Vendor shall indemnify Customer against covered third-party claims, subject to a separate $300,000 limit.

§ 9Limitation of Liability

Aggregate liability shall not exceed $100,000. Indemnification is addressed in Section 8.

2
Check severity: medium

Incident notice doesn't point to the Notices section

Section 7 requires notice but doesn't say it must follow the delivery rules in Section 6. The check flags the missing link. Whether the general Notices section applies anyway is for the reviewer to decide.

Check severity: low

The general liability limit mentions indemnity

Section 9 mentions indemnification without saying whether indemnity sits under that limit or outside it (a carveout). The reviewer needs to settle how the general limit relates to the separate indemnity limit in Section 8.

Human review is still required.

Still open: what starts the 24-hour clock, which claims are covered, and whether the limits reflect the intended allocation of risk.

The switcher only swaps between the two saved results. It sends no clause text anywhere, including to an AI service.

Keep the words in view

Each finding points to its clause and to the clause it relies on. A reviewer reads the actual wording instead of a detached summary.

Show the connection

The duty to give notice and the rules for delivering it can sit in different sections. The link between them is part of what a reviewer needs to see.

⋯

Keep the unanswered part

A fixed rule can spot the pattern it was written for. It cannot interpret the clause, check the facts, weigh the business tradeoff, or sign off. Here, even the revised incident clause leaves the notice clock’s starting event unspecified.

What actually ran

On 19 September 2026, two checks that already exist in Steno ran over four clauses entered by hand as structured data. Steno did not read a document file or build the clause connections itself, and neither check uses an AI model. The revised notice clause was picked up from its wording alone.

The result

Two findings on the initial draft, none from the same two checks on the proposed edits.
