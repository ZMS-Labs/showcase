Steno · Contract review

Keep the contract
and its context together.

I work with contracts. I wanted a way to ask what an agreement would do in a particular situation, then follow the answer back to its language. Steno brings the document, its connections and the questions under review into one place. The legal decisions stay with the person reviewing it.

In plain terms.

A reported vendor incident can bring notice obligations, indemnity language and liability limits into the same review. Reading each clause in isolation makes it harder to see how the wording interacts.

What this page shows is an archived design of the workspace, the emblem Steno uses to show a grade and what backs it (the Hallmark), and two of Steno's drafting checks run on four made-up clauses: two problems found before the edits, none after. A drafting check is a small fixed rule that looks for one drafting pattern in a clause.

An interface with its own visual rules.

I wanted the matter workspace to give the document and its context a stable home. The archived design does that with translucent glass panels, a serif display face, plain sans-serif controls and monospace details.

The Matter Map has a Layers rail, a graph and an Inspector. Each visual cue carries one meaning, so evidence and uncertainty can be read separately:

Fill · severityBorder · tierShape · node kindHue · engine

The prototype's engine rail lists Catala, L4, Symboleo and Stipula, each marked not run. That list is part of the archived design. The tools the build used are described under research below.

The document workstation puts the selected clause's context and the review choices beside the editor.

A hallmark for the claim. Marks for its support.

The Hallmark is Steno's emblem for a grade and what backs it. The design borrows from the stamp an assayer (someone who tests metal) strikes into metal: a tablet for the grade, and a separate mark for each kind of check behind it.

Grade ladder: metal and crown facets mark the grade, from Breach up to Diamond. Uncovered sits outside the ladder.Current components · made-up inputs

Grade through material and cut

The original vocabulary runs from Breach through Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum and Diamond. Metal, crown facets and rank marks set the grades apart. “Uncovered” is a separate ungraded state.

Evidence in distinct settings

Four colored medallions and custom glyphs name Engine, Cross-check, Exception and Authority. Empty settings keep absent marks visible. Adding a mark does not promote the grade.

Recognition at two scales

A compact row chip carries the emblem, grade and rank marks. The full-size version adds room for what the grade covers, what backs it up and the trail of proof behind it, and shows a clear pending state when there is no verification yet.

A second check is not automatically independent.

The Cross-check mark means a second check agreed with the first. That second check reads the same extracted facts, so its agreement shows the two are consistent, not that the result was independently confirmed.

Grade words such as “Exemplary” and “Unimpeachable”, and the title “Assay Certificate”, are the interface's own vocabulary, not legal conclusions.

A decision and its tradeoff.

Give the design and the behavior their own evidence.

Constraint
A small drafting-check example could explain a rule, but it couldn't show the workspace's look or how a review is meant to flow.
Choice
Keep the archived map and workstation prototype, and put the real drafting-check example beside it with its own label.
Tradeoff
Readers see two exhibits instead of one apparently integrated demo, each labeled for what it shows.
Result
The design can be seen at full resolution and in motion, and the two-check result can be checked against its recorded data.

Follow the drafting example.

These steps follow the example from a made-up incident to one round of edits.

Start with a fictional incident.

The example brings notice, indemnity and liability language into one review. The four clauses were entered by hand as structured data, with no connections between them supplied. Steno did not read a document or work out those connections itself. In the revised draft, the check picks up the link to the Notices section from the wording alone.

Four relevant clauses

Notices §6, incident notice §7, indemnity §8, and limitation of liability §9.

Look at the two findings.

One check noticed that the incident-notice clause (§7) creates a notice duty without pointing to the Notices section (§6), which says how notice is delivered. The other noticed that the general liability limit (§9) mentions indemnity without saying whether indemnity sits inside that limit or outside it.

Two recorded findings

The checks rated the notice finding medium and the indemnity finding low.

Make explicit illustrative edits.

Add a Notices cross-reference. State that the general $100,000 limit excludes indemnity, which has its own $300,000 limit.

Zero findings from the same two checks

The dollar figures are made up for illustration, not recommended terms.

Keep the human questions open.

Someone still needs to judge what starts the notice clock, which claims are covered, and how the risk is split.

A narrower result than approval

Zero findings from two checks does not clear an agreement.

What the AI did
AI tools write the code. The workspace design came out of work in a design tool, Claude Design, and the Hallmark started from its own Claude Design brief. AI agents ran the literature searches described below. For this page, AI tools rendered the designs and ran the two drafting checks.

How I work with AI tools

How the design was researched and built.

Steno started with contract questions and with published research. I had AI agents work through papers collected in Zotero, a reference manager. One recorded sweep ran 46 searches and had to look for evidence against the idea as well as for it. The agents then checked in Scite, a citation database, whether later papers supported or disputed them. Scite's quota ran out partway through, and the papers it hadn't reached were marked unverified.

At its center, Steno is designed to check an agreement's structured parts against a library of positions, using fixed rules that give the same result every time. Language models only help at the edges, for example sorting documents or putting findings into plain English. A model never decides what changes in a document, which redline to use, or whether a result goes out.

For some hand-picked clauses, the design adds three existing tools made by others, each for one narrow question: what duties a clause creates and whether one is breached (eFLINT), whether a hand-built model of the clause is sound (Catala), and whether an exception overrides an obligation (clingo). Recorded runs from May 2026 used each tool in the private build on one clause family, limitation of liability. Doing this for any clause in ordinary prose is future work.

The build used Epistemic Skills, the set of working methods for AI agents shown elsewhere on this site. Its adversarial review step, where AI reviewers try to break a claim, caught an earlier write-up overstating what Catala verifies; the correction is kept in the record.

Evidence and limits.

What is real here

On 19 September 2026, two of Steno's drafting checks ran on four clauses entered by hand: two findings before the edits, none after. Neither check uses an AI model, and none of the three outside tools (eFLINT, Catala, clingo) ran.

Nothing else in Steno ran for this page, including its editor and its release steps. The workspace images and the recording show the archived prototype, and the Hallmark boards show the current emblem component. Everything on this page, including the four clauses, is made up, and no real agreement or matter appears here.

The research account and the May 2026 tool runs come from Steno's private records, summarized in the research record below. Steno's source is private, and the project is unfinished.
