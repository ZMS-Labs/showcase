Interleaf · Contract review

A contract workstation that keeps the question next to the words.

Answering a contract question usually means hunting through the agreement for every clause that touches it. Interleaf keeps the document, its connections and the open questions in one place, and checks the wording with fixed rules that give the same answer every time. The legal judgment stays with the reviewer.

Prototype · two drafting checks recorded September 19, 2026

A clause is only part of the answer.

A sample agreement, four clauses, two of Interleaf's drafting checks. Each check is a small fixed rule that looks for one drafting pattern.

  1. § 7 · Security incident

    A notice duty with no delivery rule.

    "Party A shall provide written notice of a security incident within 24 hours." Fine, but notice to whom, and how? Section 7 doesn't say.

  2. § 6 · Notices

    The delivery rules live in another section.

    Section 6 says notices go by email to each party's designated address. Nothing connects the incident notice to it. The first check flags exactly that gap.

  3. §§ 8 and 9 · Indemnity and the cap

    A cap that doesn't say what it covers.

    The general cap is $100,000. The indemnity has its own $300,000 limit. Section 9 mentions indemnification without saying whether it sits inside the cap or outside it. The second check flags that.

  4. Two edits, and both checks come back clean.

    Section 7 now says notice goes "in accordance with the Notices article." Section 9 now carves indemnification out of the general cap and points to the separate limit. Same two checks: two findings before, none after.

  5. The judgment stays with the person.

    What starts the 24-hour clock? Which claims are covered? Do the limits reflect the deal the parties meant to make? A fixed rule can spot the pattern it was written for. It can't answer those, and it isn't supposed to.

A grade should show what backs it.

The Hallmark is Interleaf's mark for a grade and, separately, the kinds of evidence behind it. It's modeled on the stamp an assayer strikes into metal. The grade and the supporting marks are kept apart, so evidence never inflates a grade.

Fixed rules at the center. Language models only at the edges.

At its core, Interleaf is designed to check an agreement's structured parts against a library of positions, using rules that give the same result every time. Language models help at the edges, such as sorting documents or putting findings into plain English. A model never decides what changes in a document, which redline to use, or whether a result goes out.

For some hand-picked clauses, the design adds three existing tools built by others, each for one narrow question: what duties a clause creates and whether one is breached (eFLINT), whether a hand-built model of a clause is sound (Catala), and whether an exception overrides an obligation (clingo). Recorded runs from May 2026 used each one in the private build on one clause family, limitation of liability.

The design started from research. AI agents worked through papers collected in a reference manager; one recorded sweep ran 46 searches and had to look for evidence against the idea as well as for it. The agents then checked how later papers received each source. When the citation service's quota ran out partway through, the papers it hadn't reached were marked unverified rather than assumed.

The build used Epistemic Skills. In one review, AI reviewers tried to break a claim and caught an earlier write-up overstating what Catala verifies. The correction is kept in the record.

What went into this work.

What I did

I decide what each project is for and check what comes back. I work with contracts, and Interleaf is the tool I wanted: the contract and its matter in one place, with connections you can follow and the legal decisions left to the reviewer. It's built around a rule I hold for AI on legal work: a model never decides what changes in a document.

What the AI did

AI tools write the code. They built the workspace, rendered the exhibits and ran the two recorded drafting checks. The Hallmark started from a design brief in a dedicated Claude Design session.

The full record: inputs, provenance and limits

The revised notice clause was recognized from its wording alone; this isn't evidence that a parser extracted or resolved the connections. eFLINT, Catala and clingo didn't run for this example. The recorded party labels were replaced with Party A and Party B for presentation; the rest of the clause wording and the findings are unchanged. The amounts, the 24-hour period and the edits are illustrative, not recommended terms.

Interleaf's source is private, so these records are what you can check. The Hallmark notes record that the cross-check shares extracted facts with the primary check, so independence isn't implied, and that grade words are design vocabulary, not legal conclusions.

The research record notes the correction on Catala: an earlier write-up described it as verifying a clause; a design review narrowed that to checking that hand-built models are structurally sound.
