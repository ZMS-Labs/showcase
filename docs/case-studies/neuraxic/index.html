Neuraxic · Writing story worlds

I wanted a place where a story’s prose, people, places and rules could develop together. I’m shaping Neuraxic around that idea: start with any fragment, explore its connections, and try another version without losing track of what I’ve already decided.

In plain terms.

In Neuraxic, nothing counts as settled in a story until the author accepts it. Alongside the app’s screens, this page shows five tests that passed on two questions: what a version of the story can see, and what the author has accepted.

Original interface · fictional content

One idea, several ways to see it.

These are four of the app’s own screens: a shelf of worlds, a draft with suggested details, a map of relationships and a list of claims. The interface is real; only the data is made up. All four show the same invented setting, The Tideglass Reach.

Neuraxic keeps a story’s facts as claims, single statements such as “the lantern falls dark as the water recedes.” Each claim is either a proposal or canon. An open proposal is something to consider. Canon is what the author has explicitly accepted. A detail appearing on screen does not make that decision for them. On screen, an open seal marks a proposal and a gold seal marks canon.

The screens use the app’s current code, unchanged, at version f29176bf. Two planned features are still placeholders in that version: a cousin copy, meant to be a copy in another world that grows into its own thing, and sharing one element across several worlds. See the interface study record (JSON).

A fragment can find its place later.

I chose an idea-first workspace. A “loose leaf” is an unplaced element; a “tome” groups a universe. The Library holds tomes, while the Catalog also includes loose elements.

Open an element and it becomes the focus. Prose, claims and the neighborhood map are views of that same subject, so writing and world-building can stay connected. Lenses are different ways to read its relationships.

The Claims screen states the approval rule in the app’s own words: “A proposal becomes canon only when you seal it. Every seal writes a receipt.” The Seal button runs the same step the tests further down call promotion.

Where I want to take it.

The wider design distinguishes world truth, the events a story presents, and what characters or audiences believe. A character can be wrong while the author knows the answer.

Time and versions

When an event happens in the fiction differs from when the author records it. An alternate version starts from a chosen point in the main story.

Contradictions worth exploring

A broken rule might become a mystery, an exception or a revision; the author decides which. A shared story structure could later support adaptations without silently changing the underlying world.

These are design ambitions. The tests below cover a small part of them: branching a version, flagging one kind of conflict and recording an approval.

A lamplit archive where the marks carry meaning.

Dark green surfaces, parchment-toned text and warm gold give the app the feel of a place to read and think. Its shelves and dossiers make a collection of story facts approachable.

The marks have specific meanings: the open and gold seals separate proposals from canon at a glance, and ember-colored findings draw attention to concerns.

What belongs in this version of the story?

The five tests answer two questions: what can a version of the story see, and what has the author accepted? Neuraxic calls each version of a story’s facts a continuity. The diagrams explain the tests with a second setting, Bellweather Reach, separate from The Tideglass Reach in the screens above. They are not a recording of the app. Step 1 asks what had been recorded as of an earlier moment. Steps 2 and 3 follow a what-if version as it splits off, step 4 keeps a contradiction open, and step 5 records an approval.

Keep a conflict available for judgment.

A different test starts with a canon claim, then records a claim that gives a different value for the same detail of the same thing in the same version.

In the example, “the beacon is unlit” conflicts with the canon claim that it is lit. Neuraxic reports the conflict and keeps the new claim as a proposal. The idea stays available, and canon stays unchanged.

The beacon is lit.

The beacon is unlit.

Green marks canon; the plain box is the proposal.

Record promotion as its own action.

When the author promotes a proposal, Neuraxic writes a canon receipt that records who did it. If the same promotion is sent again with the same identifying key, Neuraxic returns the original receipt.

One test saves the first promotion, then starts fresh before retrying. The receipt still matches.

A claim the author chooses to promote

A receipt records the decision without judging whether the story detail is good or coherent.

The branching tests cover a story’s own claims. Material a story pulls in from a shared library follows different rules, and those rules were not part of these tests.

What the rules cost the author.

A stable starting point needs deliberate updates.

A what-if keeps the point where it split off, so a useful later correction to the main story doesn’t reach it on its own. Bringing a correction across is not shown here.

A conflict needs a decision.

Keeping a disputed proposal lets the author reconsider it. Deciding which version of the detail is right, and whether the story still holds together, is still up to the author.

What went into this work.

How I work with AI tools

What the five tests checked.

Five tests passed on 19 September 2026, with zero failures and zero skips.

Asking at an earlier point in the record leaves later claims out of the answer.

A what-if version keeps the claim it started with and does not see a claim added to the main story after the split, while the main story does.

A claim that gives a different value for the same detail as canon is reported and stays a proposal.

Promotion records who did it, and sending the same promotion twice returns one receipt, even after the first is saved and a new session is opened.

What is real here

On 19 September 2026, the five tests ran the app’s real backend code against a throwaway database, with the network switched off. No AI model ran and nothing was generated.

The screens and the recording were prepared the same day. The interface in them is the app’s own code; the made-up data was fed to the screens as sample answers instead of coming from the app’s backend. The screens made twelve requests, all of them reads, and no errors appeared. Both settings, The Tideglass Reach and Bellweather Reach, were written for this page, and no private story material appears on it.

These five tests do not show how the app behaves on its real database under load, how sign-in works, whether it is ready to deploy, how the full interface behaves end to end, whether the writing inside it is any good, or whether every kind of contradiction in a world gets caught. The app’s source code and the full test record stay private.
